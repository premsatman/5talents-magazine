# You can't get in without walking through the job fair

**Standfirst:** A one-day leadership conference in Hyderabad on 31 October has been designed so that nobody reaches the stage without passing a job fair first. ₹399, doors at half past eight.

---

Most conferences hide the stalls. You come for the stage, and the exhibitors sit somewhere off to one side hoping you will wander over between sessions. Most people never do.

The Mighty Dream has done the opposite. At Sound Barrier, their annual conference on Saturday 31 October, the route from the registration desk runs through a trade show, into a job fair, and only then into the stadium. You leave the same way. Whatever else happens that day, everyone in the building will walk past employers and founders twice.

That is a small decision about floor plans, and it is also an argument. Most of our readers have been taught, quietly and without anyone saying it outright, that their working life and their faith belong in separate files. One is Sunday. The other is the thing that pays. A conference that makes you walk through the job fair to reach the worship is saying something about that, and saying it in the only language a building can speak.

## What it is

Sound Barrier is The Mighty Dream Conference 2026, held at the Gachibowli Indoor Stadium in Hyderabad from nine in the morning until five. Doors open at half past eight. Tickets are ₹399.

Across the day there are four sessions, a panel, worship, a job fair, a trade show, and a set of breakout conversations with industry leaders that they call Collide. The organisers divide the people they are expecting into five groups: creatives, students, ministry leaders, business owners and entrepreneurs, and corporate professionals.

The theme is *Echo the past. Sound the future.*

"When we look at the legacy left by the Christian community," the organisers say, "we see that there were some world-changing pioneers who stood their ground and walked with boldness and accomplished what they were called for. But in the last few decades we do not see the same happening."

The echo, then, is not of what those people built. It is of what they carried: "the values, the commitment and the courage." The second half of the phrase is the part aimed at the room. *Sound the future* means "speaking into reality a future that is unique to their own purpose and calling."

Reinhard Komanapalli, who founded The Mighty Dream, puts the day's intent more plainly. He expects people to leave with "a sense of knowing the built-in power of their tongue," and with the understanding that they have "the same built-in architecture to build things just like God did." He also expects them to leave able "to cancel out negative voices and noises both in and around them."

## The shape of the day

Registration opens at 8:30, and then the walk through the stalls. There are crowd activities and giveaways to start. Worship begins at ten sharp and runs for forty-five minutes, led by Sam Alex Pasula, Phebe Bright and Tali Angh.

The first session is Ben Komanapalli Jr., founder of one of the fastest-growing churches in Hyderabad, who is invited every year to bring the pastoral reading of the theme. Tea, then Justin Owens, whose subject is the unglamorous end of leadership — habits, systems, the decisions that turn an intention into a thing that exists.

Then lunch, and lunch is where the day gets interesting.

## The tables

The industry leaders do not get a session. They get the lunch hour, and they are already sitting at their tables when the stadium empties. You collect your food, look at the names, and walk to whichever table you want. Then you ask.

This is what Collide is. "These are not the typical breakout rooms," the organisers say. "These are much more personal where the attendees sit or stand around the table and ask as many questions as possible. This is like a quick mentoring session."

Their instruction to anyone coming is worth repeating in full:

> "Walk in with all your questions and not just your resume. Ask questions! Yes, the resume is important, but the more you interact the more you get to know about the job market, the level at which companies are hiring, the future of that role or that company."

And then, almost as an aside: "Last year we had one of the social media volunteers receive a job offer from one of the businesses, so you never know what door is waiting to be opened."

That is the sentence to hold on to. A volunteer — somebody working the event, not attending it — walked out with a job. Nobody put that on a poster.

After lunch there is twenty minutes of praise, then a panel of three Indian entrepreneurs who built their brands themselves. Reinhard takes the final session, and there is a challenge to the room. The conference next year is announced before anyone goes home.

The job fair and the trade show run the whole time.

## Where it came from

Reinhard carried the idea for about a decade before anything happened with it. The Mighty Dream only came into being in 2024. In the two years since, it has run conferences and worship nights across the country, and this year took its Pioneer Again tour to nine cities: Dimapur, Chandrapur, Nagpur, Thane, Mumbai, Pune, Jabalpur, Chennai and Trivandrum. More than 2,300 people came to its events over the year.

Reinhard runs it with Achsa Komanapalli, co-founder and director. Their stated aim is to reach a hundred thousand people by 2030 — not as an audience figure, but as a count of people who treat calling as a compass rather than a career path.

There is a line in their own material that sits oddly against a stadium booking, and is better for it: *leadership is not built on stages, it is built in obscurity.* A day like this one is not where the work happens. On their own account, it is where somebody finds out what the work is.

If you are in Hyderabad on the 31st, go early. The tables fill up.
