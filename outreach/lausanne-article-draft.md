## The Language People Pray In

Some years ago I began writing a daily devotional. It was a small thing. One short reading each morning, written plainly, aimed at young people who had grown up in church and quietly stopped understanding what was said there.

I expected it to be read by people like the ones I knew in Hyderabad. It was not, or not only. [CONFIRM: how many people currently read PS.Today daily or monthly, and how many countries.] What surprised me was not the reach. It was the letters.

They came from people whose English was their third or fourth language, thanking me for writing simply. Not because the theology was simple, but because the sentences were. They had been reading Christian material in English for years and understanding perhaps two thirds of it, and had assumed the missing third was their own fault.

That is the observation this article is about, and I think it matters for anyone working in prayer mobilisation.

### Prayer has a language problem that mission has largely solved

Bible translation is one of the great achievements of the modern church. We have understood for two centuries that Scripture must reach people in the language they think in. [CONFIRM: current figure for languages with at least some Scripture, from Wycliffe or the Bible Society, with the year.] Enormous effort, enormous cost, and rightly so.

Prayer mobilisation has not made the same journey.

Look at what is available online to someone who wants to pray for the unreached. Prayer guides, prayer calendars, prayer apps, daily prompts, maps, statistics. Almost all of it is in English, with a handful of major languages added where budgets allowed. We have translated the Word into the languages of the world and then asked the world to pray about it in ours.

I do not think anybody decided this. It is simply what happens when the people producing prayer resources and the people who most need to use them are not the same people.

### The problem is not hostility, it is indexing

There is a second thing that is easy to miss if you have not tried to publish for these audiences.

We tend to describe the 10/40 window in terms of access and resistance. Closed countries, restricted access, hostile governments. All of that is real. But there is a quieter obstacle that has nothing to do with hostility.

Online, you are found by search. Search is dominated by languages with commercial weight. If you write in a language that has few speakers online and little commercial value, you are not blocked, you are simply not surfaced. A believer in a minority-language community may have a phone, data, and no restriction at all on what they read, and still never encounter a page written for them, because no algorithm has any reason to show it to them.

That is a different problem from persecution and it has a different solution. Persecution requires courage. This requires somebody to publish deliberately in languages that will never repay the effort commercially.

### What we learned building for readers who do not read English easily

Three things, offered because they cost us time to learn and may save somebody else the same time.

**Write short sentences before you translate anything.** The instinct is to write normally and then translate. But complicated English translates into complicated everything else, and machine translation multiplies the damage. We found that simplifying the English first improved every version downstream, and improved the English too. [CONFIRM: whether PS.Today is currently in more than one language, and which.]

**A daily rhythm outperforms a large library.** People did not want an archive. They wanted one thing, today, that they could finish. This seems obvious and yet most of what we build in mission is a resource collection rather than a habit.

**Ask who is exposed by the prayer, not only who is prayed for.** This one changed how we think. Prayer content for the 10/40 window is usually written from the perspective of the person praying safely elsewhere. But if the resource is any good, believers inside those regions will use it too. A page that names a country, a people group and a specific difficulty is a page that could sit in the browser history of somebody for whom that is not a neutral fact. Anyone building in this space has to design for the safety of the reader, not only the sensitivity of the subject.

### What we are attempting

Out of this we are now building a prayer page for the 10/40 window, written for and eventually in the languages of the people it concerns rather than only about them.

I want to be honest that it is early. [CONFIRM: exact current status. Is anything live, in build, or still in planning? Say so plainly. Do not let this paragraph imply more than exists.] I am writing about it now not because it is finished but because I suspect several other people are quietly attempting the same thing, and we would all do better work together than separately.

The ambition is modest and specific. One short prayer each day. Written simply enough to translate well. Published in languages nobody would publish in for commercial reasons. Designed so that a believer inside the window can use it without it costing them anything.

### An invitation rather than a conclusion

I have no results to report yet, and I would rather say that than dress up a plan as an achievement.

But I would like to end with a question for anyone reading this who works in prayer mobilisation, translation, or digital mission. If Scripture in the heart language has been our great labour for two hundred years, why has prayer in the heart language not been?

I do not think it is because anyone decided against it. I think it is because it has been nobody's particular job. If it is yours, or you would like it to be, I would be glad to hear from you.
