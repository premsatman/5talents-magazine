# Africa — 50 candidates

Researched 15 September 2026, five parallel searches: musicians, authors and public figures, pastors, NGO and social enterprise, seminary faculty.

**Same rule as the Philippines.** Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana and Zambia are heavily Christian, so nationality proves nothing. Every person is classified by whether faith actually shapes the work.

**One extra rule for this region: prosperity gospel.** Every search was told to flag and exclude it. Across all fifty, the checks came back clean — the sets skew Catholic, Anglican, Methodist, Baptist, Reformed, Lutheran and evangelical, deliberately away from that world. Zambia's Conrad Mbewe is a well-known public critic of it, and Ghana's Kofi Owusu Peprah said on the record that you cannot put a price on the cross.

## 3. Musicians

Eight countries, Nigeria held to three.

**Kofi Owusu Peprah, Ghana** is the standout. He resigned from Ghana's national intelligence bureau when ordered to choose between the job and the music. His words: arresting souls for God through music has become his new mission. And on money: you cannot put a price on the cross. That last line is the best anti-prosperity quote found anywhere in this research. Email is snippet-only, verify first.

**Neon Adejo, Nigeria** was raised Muslim, converted, went from homelessness to worship leadership, now signed to Tribl. The sharpest cost-of-conversion story on the list, and the hardest to reach now he has a label.

**Guardian Angel, Kenya** was swept off the streets as a homeless nine-year-old and chose to seek God in a police station. 

**Levixone, Uganda** carries the rawest story: he has said publicly that his mother was gang-raped and he was born of it. Handle with great care if he says yes.

**Janet Manyowa, Zimbabwe** is the only verified contact in this set and hosts a TV show interviewing people about their faith. At 41 she is older than our band, but she is reachable and professional.

Also: Greatman Takit and Limoblaze, Nigeria. Pompi, Zambia, criticised at home for performing gospel in nightclubs, which is itself a good question. Peace Hozy, Rwanda, 15K followers, years as someone else's backup voice. Bucy Radebe, South Africa, marked **Stated not Operative** — no first-person faith quote was found, so do not oversell her.

## 5. Pastors and ministry leaders

**This is the weakest set for contacts and the strongest for stories.** Not one personal email was found across ten people. Most will need a denominational or partner-organisation intermediary, and several have no locatable website at all.

**Allan Ngwaba, DRC** is the most urgent story on the entire African list. He gives daily trauma counselling to civilians in Goma under rebel occupation. Verified across several outlets. No contact of any kind.

**Rev. Buba Aliyu, Nigeria** was born Muslim into a Fulani herding family, converted in 1995, was disowned, and his own brother nearly shot him before their mother pushed the gun away. He now evangelises in no-go zones and is a marked man. He is over 50, and his claim of a million converts is self-reported and must be caveated in print.

**John Deng, Uganda** is a South Sudanese refugee pastoring a congregation that deliberately brings Nuer and Dinka together inside a settlement of 125,000 — the two groups whose war he fled.

**Francis Mbuvi, Kenya** was a Nairobi street child and now runs the ministry that rescued him. Only contactable route in the whole set: info@madeinthestreets.org, a screened general inbox.

**Olivier Ndizeye, Rwanda** planted a church at Ntarama, the site of a 1994 genocide massacre that is now a memorial holding victims' remains.

Also: Muzemil Biffo, Ethiopia. Sibusiso Mlotshwa, South Africa, building a deliberately anti-prosperity church in Mamelodi. Salim Mpambanya, Zambia, whose own church doubted he was old enough. Kofi Gyansah, Ghana. **Rebecca Maduley Kurubai, Tanzania**, reportedly the first ordained Maasai woman in her diocese who grew a plant from 40 to 1,000 in a year — the best unverified lead here and worth a dedicated check.

## 6. NGO and social enterprise

**Charles Mulli, Kenya** has the single best faith quote found in the whole African search: it is not me, it is Christ working through me. Himself an abandoned child who begged on the streets, he gave up a business empire in 1989 and has taken in 35,000 children since. He is 77, so pair him with one of his adult children now running campuses.

**Isabelle Kamariza, Rwanda** is the cleanest ordinary-person story. Solid'Africa began in a prayer group when she proposed praying for the sick. Now 1,500 hospital meals a day, five million served. Verified inbox, low sensitivity, excellent question for a young reader: what do you do with a nudge to help one person, before you know it becomes an organisation?

**Peter Sewakiryanga, Uganda** rescues children who survived ritual sacrifice. Verified operative faith on the organisation's own site. **Highest consent stakes on the list** — any survivor material must go through their safeguarding process, never improvised.

**Cheryl Allen, South Africa**, Door of Hope, the original baby-box ministry, 1,946 babies and 902 adoptions. The most explicitly Christian voice in the set. Beneficiaries are abandoned infants who cannot consent at all.

**Faith Liyena, Zambia** told her husband about her calling to care for orphans before she agreed to marry him. Thirty-two years running.

**Marked NOT VERIFIED, do not present as faith stories:** Ruth Benson-Idahosa of Pathfinders, whose organisation's own materials are entirely secular; Emi-Beth Quantson of Kawa Moka, Ghana, an excellent coffee entrepreneur with no faith language anywhere; Beth Koigi of Majik Water, Kenya. All three need the faith question asked directly before anything is written.

**Foreign-founded, say so if published:** Starfish Malawi, still British-chaired. Project Mercy, Ethiopia, Ethiopian-American co-founded.
