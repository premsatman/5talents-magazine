# Rewriting the Missionary Story column for 2026

Written 30 August 2026, after three pieces in this column were found to be
copied from Wikipedia and from a commercial site (see `articles/TRIAGE.md`).
Roughly fifteen more are still ahead in the archive.

The column has two problems, and only one of them is copying. The other is that
a column called "Missionary Story", written the way it was written in 2012, is
the wrong shape for the magazine you are relaunching in India in 2026 — legally,
editorially, and for the readers you actually want.

This is the replacement.

---

## 1. Where the law actually sits

**Not legal advice.** Get an Indian media lawyer to read this page once and mark
it up. What follows is the reasoning behind the editorial rules, not a legal
opinion, and the acts change every year — Maharashtra became the thirteenth
state in March 2026.

States with a Freedom of Religion Act in force or passed: Arunachal Pradesh,
Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Karnataka, Madhya
Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh. Telangana,
where the magazine is published, has none. Your readers do not stay inside
Telangana.

What the acts criminalise is an **act**: causing a conversion by force, fraud,
misrepresentation, coercion, undue influence or *allurement*. What they do not
criminalise is writing about faith. Article 25 protects the right to propagate,
which the courts have read as the right to transmit and expound your beliefs;
the operative test on a conversion is whether it was voluntary.

So publishing a testimony is not the offence. The real exposure is narrower and
worth naming precisely, because it changes what we write rather than whether we
write:

- **A published account can become somebody else's evidence.** If a piece says
  a named Indian person was converted, and names or clearly identifies whoever
  brought them, that page is a document a complainant can carry to a police
  station — against them, not against us. Several of these acts also reverse the
  burden of proof onto the accused.
- **"Allurement" is undefined and elastic.** Its vagueness is the main ground of
  the constitutional challenges. Faith content sitting next to an offer of help,
  money, schooling or medical care can be read as the two being connected.
- **The chilling effect is the point of the vagueness.** Which means the sane
  response is not silence — it is writing that gives a hostile reader nothing to
  work with, while giving an honest reader everything.

### The three hard rules

1. **Never describe a conversion that happened in India, involving a named
   living person, with the persuader identified.** Any one of those three alone
   is usually fine. Together they are a document.
2. **Keep faith content and aid content on separate pages.** No testimony inside
   a piece about the magazine's charitable work, and no charitable appeal inside
   a testimony. That separation is the single cheapest protection against an
   allurement reading, and it is also just better journalism.
3. **Overseas is different.** A figure whose conversion work happened outside
   India — Müller in Bristol, Luther in Saxony, Wesley in England, Booth in
   London — can be written about fully, conversions included. The acts are state
   laws about conduct in India. Say plainly where and when the events happened
   and the distinction takes care of itself.

### First-person is the safe register

Someone describing **their own** change of belief is protected speech and is
also the most honest form the material takes. "I decided" is a person exercising
a right. "He converted them" is an allegation about a third party. Almost every
risky sentence in the old column can be fixed by moving it into the subject's
own mouth and keeping the third party out of it.

Bhaskar Rao's interview is the model. He says a pastor enrolled him in a school
and that he later came to faith himself. The two facts are not welded into a
transaction, because in his telling they were not one.

## 2. What the column becomes

**Rename it.** "Missionary Story" announces a genre, tells the reader what to
think before the first line, and is the exact phrase a hostile search picks up.
It also undersells the material — these are biographies of people who built
things.

Candidates: **Lives** · **The Ones Before Us** · **What They Built** ·
**Long Obedience**. It sits in Heritage either way.

**Widen the subject list.** The 2012 column ran almost entirely on white
European men, which is neither the readership nor the truth about Indian
Christian history. Prefer, in this order:

- Indians, especially Indian women — Pandita Ramabai, Chundra Lela, Sadhu Sundar
  Singh, the Bible translators nobody has heard of
- People who **built** something countable — schools, hospitals, presses,
  dictionaries, orphanages
- The less famous over the famous. A tenth piece on Luther is worth less than a
  first piece on Ramabai's famine work at Mukti
- Recent and living people, handled under the rules above

## 3. The seven-beat structure

Every piece, 900–1,600 words, in this order. Go past 1,300 only when the life
genuinely carries it — Müller needed 1,600 and Ramabai will too; most will not. The beats are the brief you
asked for — history, childhood, what they did, why they are famous, testimony,
achievement, and what it says to a reader in 2026.

**1. Open on a scene or a number, never a birth date.**
"By the age of ten he was stealing his father's tax money" beats "George Müller
was born on 27 September 1805". The dates go in beat two, where they belong.

**2. Childhood, and what formed them.**
The specific conditions: the family, the money, what the country was like. Two
hundred words. This is the beat a fourteen-year-old reader attaches to.

**3. The turn — in their own words wherever a source allows.**
What changed inside them, quoted from an autobiography, a letter or a diary if
one survives. First person. No third party doing anything to them.

**4. The work, with receipts.**
What they actually made, in numbers: 10,024 orphans, 117 schools, £100,000 of
building, 285,407 Bibles. Young readers are sceptical of claims and persuaded by
figures. This is the longest beat.

**5. The cost.**
Every one of these lives has a hard passage — the deaths, the debts, the
opponents, the failures, the parts that did not work. Leave it in. A story with
no cost reads as advertising, and this generation switches off at the first
whiff of it.

**6. Why they are still known.**
What survived them, and what the honest criticism is. If the figure did
something indefensible — and Luther did — say so in a sentence and move on.
Hagiography is not credibility.

**7. "So what, in 2026?" — one closing section, 150 words, explicit.**
Not sprinkled through the piece as moralising. One place, at the end, where the
magazine says what this asks of a reader now. Müller refusing to fundraise, in
an economy built on asking strangers for money, is a genuinely strange and
interesting idea. That is the register: interesting, not instructive.

## 4. Writing for readers born after 2010

Your Gen Alpha readers are in school. Gen Z are at university or in their first
jobs. What actually works:

- **One idea per paragraph, three or four sentences.** They read on a phone.
- **Numbers over adjectives.** "10,024 orphans" lands; "a great work for God"
  does not.
- **Lead with the mess.** Thief, liar, gambler at ten — then the orphanages.
  Starting at the achievement loses them in the first line.
- **No triumphalism, no "mighty man of God".** They can smell being sold to from
  a great distance, and this vocabulary is exactly what triggers it.
- **Answer "why should I care" out loud.** They will not do that work for you,
  and they are not being lazy — they are being asked for their attention by a
  thousand things.
- **Never flatten the person into a lesson.** The moment a life becomes a moral,
  the reader stops believing the life was real.

Nothing here means dumbing down. It means Müller's accounting practice is more
interesting than Müller's piety, and it is also more true to him.

## 5. Sources

**Never** paraphrase Wikipedia or any living writer's article. Go to what
Wikipedia's own footnotes cite — those sources are usually out of copyright and
always better.

For Müller specifically, all public domain and all online:

- *Autobiography of George Müller* (Centenary Memorial edition, 1905) — Archive.org
- A. T. Pierson, *George Müller of Bristol* (1899) — Archive.org
- W. H. Harding, *The Life of George Müller* (1914)
- Works by Müller at Project Gutenberg

Facts are not copyrightable. Sentences are. Reading three primary sources and
writing your own paragraphs is both legally clean and a better piece than any
summary.

**Every piece carries a Sources note at the foot** listing what was read. It
takes thirty seconds, it is what a magazine does, and it is the practice that
would have prevented all of this.

Before importing any archive piece that reads like reference writing, search one
distinctive full sentence from it in quotation marks. Ten minutes.

## 6. The checklist

Before a piece in this column is imported:

- [ ] Written from primary sources, not paraphrased from an article
- [ ] One distinctive sentence searched; nothing came back
- [ ] Sources note at the foot
- [ ] Byline is a real person who actually wrote it, or the magazine
- [ ] No conversion in India attributed to a named living person
- [ ] No persuader identified alongside a convert
- [ ] The subject's own change of belief is in their own words
- [ ] No aid, money, schooling or medical help mentioned near the faith content
- [ ] The cost beat is present and honest
- [ ] The criticism of the subject is acknowledged, if there is one
- [ ] A closing "so what in 2026" section exists and is under 200 words
- [ ] `archiveMeta.rightsCleared` set truthfully
