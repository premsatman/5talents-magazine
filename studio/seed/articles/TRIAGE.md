# Archive triage

One row per piece in each issue, with a verdict. Anything not marked **publish**
is deliberately absent from the issue's `.json` file — this is where the reason
lives, so the same judgment does not get re-litigated in six months.

Verdicts:

- **publish** — original 5Talents work, rights ours, in scope
- **check rights** — probably syndicated or adapted; needs a source traced first
- **out of scope** — republishable, but outside the 2026 editorial scope
- **skip** — mostly graphic, or too thin to stand as a web article

### The suicide-method edit, and why it is your call

Pavithran's testimony as printed describes the method of his suicide attempt in
physical detail — the cutting, the blood, the water changing colour. That
description has been removed. What is kept: that he was severely depressed, that
he went to the lake, that he tried to end his life, that he lost consciousness,
that he prayed, that he woke in a hospital bed. Nothing about the arc is softened.

The reason is not squeamishness. Method detail in a published account measurably
raises risk among readers who are already vulnerable — it is the one element
every safe-messaging guideline, WHO's included, asks publishers to leave out,
and the effect is strongest with young readers, who are the audience 5Talents is
being relaunched for. A magazine can carry the whole weight of a testimony
without carrying the instructions.

Three things follow from that decision, and they are yours to confirm:

1. **The article ends with a support note** and the Tele-MANAS number (14416).
   A testimony about a suicide attempt that offers no route to help is the
   version that does harm.
2. **`archiveMeta.editNote` records the change on the document**, so nobody
   later "restores" the original thinking it was lost in conversion.
3. **`interviewMeta.consentOnFile` is not set.** Pavithran is a named, living
   person and this is the most personal thing in the archive. Consent for a 2012
   print run is not consent for a permanent, indexed web page. Worth an email to
   him before this goes live — and it is a good email to send anyway, since he
   may have fourteen more years of story.

If you want the original text back, say so and it goes back — it is your
magazine and his testimony. But it should be a decision, not a default.

The Luther piece is the serious one. Wikipedia at least has a licence we could

**Both live pieces have been retracted.** `npm run retract` sets `retracted` on
the two documents with the reason recorded on each; every front-end query runs
through the `live` filter, the article page and sitemap included, so they leave
the site entirely rather than merely dropping out of the listings. The documents
stay in Sanity — deleting them would destroy the record of what was published
and for how long, which is the first thing anyone asks about afterwards.

Three things follow.

**Nothing under this byline gets published without a source check.** That means
every remaining issue: the Missionary Story column ran monthly, so there are
roughly fifteen more of these, and on the evidence the prior should be that they
are copied until shown otherwise. Ten minutes of searching per piece.

**This is not really about one person.** In 2012 a small volunteer magazine
copying an encyclopedia into a column was ordinary practice and nobody thought
of it as theft. Republishing it in 2026 on an indexed, permanent, advertising-
carrying website is a different act with different consequences, and the honest
framing when this comes up is that the standard changed, not that someone was
dishonest. Elizabeth Clayton is still credited as Creative Manager on the
masthead and may well be reachable — worth a conversation rather than a verdict.

**The two GotQuestions pieces confirm the other half of the pattern.** July's

page. Unsigned doctrinal Q&A in this archive should be assumed syndicated.

What survives all of this is the part that was always the point: the interviews.
Every Talent Search piece so far — Samuel Stanley Jones, Mary Sujani, Pavithran
Golconda, Bhaskar Rao — is original reporting, conducted by the magazine, with a
real person on the other end of it. That is the archive's actual asset, and it
is untouched.

### A GROQ trap, recorded

The retraction filter reads `coalesce(retracted, false) == false`, not the
obvious `retracted != true`. In GROQ a comparison against null evaluates to
null, and a filter treats null as false — so `retracted != true` would have
silently hidden every article predating the field, which is all of them. The
kind of bug that ships green and empties the homepage. Noted in AGENTS.md.

### Ramabai, rewritten

Written fresh from her own 1887 book and from Eric J. Sharpe's entry in the *Biographical Dictionary of Christian Missions*. Facts the copied version had wrong or missing:

- Her parents died in the **1874 famine** specifically, which is the fact that explains why she personally went out into the 1896–97 famine to collect children
- She sailed to England in 1883 **to qualify as a doctor**. Deafness ended it. Every retelling omits this, and it is the detail that shows she meant to come back useful, not to be rescued
- She was **already going deaf**, and stayed deaf, and ran Mukti anyway
- She made Sharada Sadan **explicitly non-proselytising** — a baptised Christian, funded by American Christians, who refused to require anything of the widows in her care. Her donors were uneasy and she held the line
- She taught herself **Hebrew and Greek in her sixties** and translated the Bible into Marathi from the original languages, setting the type on her own press
- Manorama died in 1921; Ramabai died nine months later

### One word to decide

The word "Hindu" appears twice in the rewritten piece, both times as the title of Ramabai's own book, *The High-Caste Hindu Woman*. Renaming someone's book is not an option, so the choice is to keep the title or to refer to it as "her first English book, published in 1887". Everywhere else the piece uses Brahmin, Sanskrit, the Puranas, caste and orthodox opinion, which is more precise writing in any case.

### The Andrew Murray reprint

Page 14 is a chapter of Andrew Murray's *Waiting on God* (1896), correctly credited to him. Murray died in 1917, so it is public domain and there is no rights problem at all — it is an honest reprint, not a copy.

It is still not our writing, and 1896 devotional prose is a hard sell to the readers this relaunch is aimed at. Left out for now. If you want a classic-reprint strand in the magazine it is a legitimate way to fill a slot, and it should be labelled as a reprint on the page.

### Images: the first good photograph in the archive, and we probably cannot use it

Page 4 carries a 2287 × 1988 photograph of Maria Clara in a garden holding a red canvas of dancing figures. It is the first image in four issues large enough for the overlay hero treatment, and it is properly lit and composed.

That is the problem. Every other archive photograph is around 560 px from a volunteer's camera. This one is a professional shoot — and the interview on the facing page says an AFP photographer took a particular interest in her paintings, and that the photographs ran in *Forbes*, *India Today* and others. Using an agency frame would be a straight repeat of the mistake this whole audit is about.

Imported as `textOnly`, no hero. **The fix is easy and worth doing:** Maria Clara is living, in Hyderabad, blogs her work, and her husband is a Times of India journalist. Ask her. She may have her own photographs of the paintings, and fourteen years of new work since.

### Carey, rewritten

Written from his own *Enquiry* and the nineteenth-century biographies. What the copied version left out, all of which is better than what it kept:

- He was apprenticed to shoemaking **because sunlight blistered his skin** and he needed an indoor trade
- Serampore was chosen because it was a **Danish colony**. The East India Company would not permit missionaries, so the whole enterprise ran for years out of a foreign enclave fifteen miles beyond British reach
- He was a **working botanist** — a five-acre garden, correspondence with Kew, published work on Indian flora, and the founding of the Agri-Horticultural Society of India in 1820
- **Serampore College**, founded 1818, chartered by Denmark to grant degrees, admitting students regardless of caste or religion. Still open
- The **1812 fire** destroyed the presses, the type for a dozen languages, and years of manuscripts including a polyglot dictionary. He started again
- When sati was prohibited in 1829 the edict reached him on a Sunday morning. **He skipped preaching and translated it into Bengali that day** so it could take effect without losing a week
- He was seventy-two when he died, not seventy-three as the source has it

And the part every version skips: **Dorothy Carey**. She did not want to go. A five-year-old son died of dysentery in a country she had no wish to be in, and she was severely mentally ill for about twelve years until her death in 1807. She is a real cost of this story and she did not choose it. It is in the rewrite.

### "Crossing the Bridge" — held on editorial grounds, not rights

The piece by "Mark" appears to be original, and it is not being held for provenance. It is being held because of what it says.

Its reading of Joshua 3 is that God physically lay down in the Jordan — "just as we do on a couch or bed" — and that the Israelites walked across the top of His body. That is not a metaphor in the writing; the author is explicit that it is what happened, and reasons from a science-lesson analogy about a wooden block displacing water.

That is an eccentric enough claim about the nature of God that publishing it under the magazine's name in 2026 would invite a doctrinal argument nobody needs. Held pending your view — it is your magazine and your theology, and it may be that the author would put it differently now.

### Fry: not confirmed copied, and replaced anyway

I could not find a source for the 2013 Fry piece, and I am not going to record it as copied on a hunch. What can be said is what it looks like. It opens with the headings *"Why is Elizabeth Fry famous?"* and *"What she did"*, and continues in short declarative sentences at roughly a seven-year-old's reading level. That is the house format of primary-school educational resources, not of a magazine.

So: **unconfirmed**. Replaced on quality grounds regardless, because a 250-word KS1 summary is not what this column should be, and Fry deserves better than she got.

She is also, incidentally, the best subject the column has picked: a woman, working entirely in England — so no Indian legal considerations at all — who built something structural rather than merely feeling strongly about it.

What the rewrite has that the original did not:

- She took her watch off before entering Newgate, so nobody would be tempted
- The **purple boots with scarlet laces** — she was a bored Quaker teenager who liked clothes, not a saint in waiting
- The Association of 1817 did not just visit. It installed a matron, opened a school, brought in **paid** needlework, and had **the women themselves discuss and vote on the rules**. That last part is the whole method
- **1818: the first woman to give evidence before a committee of the House of Commons**
- The convict ships: she got the irons stopped, and sent each woman off with needles, thread and cut patchwork — so she landed in New South Wales with something to sell and a skill to show. The *Rajah* quilt of 1841 survives in Canberra
- The Gaols Act 1823: inspections, women supervising women, and gaolers **paid a wage** instead of living off fees squeezed out of prisoners
- Her nurses' training school of 1840 supplied nurses Florence Nightingale took to the Crimea

And the cost, which the children's version naturally omits: her husband's bank failed in 1828, the family were ruined, Joseph was declared bankrupt and **disowned by the Society of Friends**. She was also criticised for the time spent away from eleven children, and several of them left the Quakers. Members of Parliament attacked her for making prison too comfortable — an argument still running two hundred years later.

### The two unsigned devotionals

"Loving the Law of God!" is unsigned and ends: *"Brethren this is the time that I write to you and extend my best wishes this week… My love is with you, until next week."* That is a page from somebody's weekly newsletter, and the register is American-preacher throughout. I could not identify the author, which is precisely the problem — an unsigned piece we cannot attribute is one we cannot defend. Not published.

"Prayer is a privilege!" is the same shape and gets the same treatment, held rather than refused outright in case you know who wrote it.

For the record, across six issues **every unsigned prose piece has turned out to be somebody else's**, or has been unattributable. That is now a reliable enough signal to treat as a rule: no byline, no publication.

### It had already been imported, so a drop script now exists

The McIntosh page was in the dataset before the decision to drop it. Removing it
from `august-2013.json` does not remove it from Sanity: the importer only
creates and updates, and has no concept of a deletion. That is deliberate, since
a file going missing should never silently unpublish anything.

Deleting it by hand in the Studio would also have failed. The August 2013 issue
holds a reference to it in `tableOfContents`, and Sanity refuses to delete a
document anything still points at. The error it gives does not name the holder.

So: `npm run drop -- <slug>`. It lists every document referencing the article,
runs dry by default, and with `--confirm` unlinks the contents entry, deletes
the article and its draft.

**It refuses to edit anything other than an issue's contents list.** If some
other document type references the article, it stops and says so rather than
patching a document it does not understand.

**Drop is not retract, and the distinction matters.** Retraction keeps the
document and hides it behind a public note, which is right for a piece that was
published and then withdrawn: the record should show something was there.
Dropping is for a piece that should never have been in the dataset, imported by
mistake or pulled before anyone saw it. If in doubt, retract, because the drop
is not reversible from the script.

### Two documents for one page: a slug rename created a duplicate

`peter-and-sandra-mcintosh` and `she-arrived-in-bangkok-and-built-a-school` are
the same page. Both were in the dataset.

**Cause.** The piece was first imported as a rewrite under the slug
`she-arrived-in-bangkok-and-built-a-school`. When Premasis asked for the printed
text back, the rewrite was replaced and the slug changed to
`peter-and-sandra-mcintosh`. The importer upserts **by slug**, so it did not
rename the existing document, it created a second one. The first was left in the
dataset, live, with the discarded rewrite in it.

**Rule: changing an article's slug after it has been imported creates a new
document and orphans the old one.** Nothing in the pipeline detects this. Either
keep the slug fixed once a piece has been imported, or drop the old document
deliberately. The safer habit is to settle the slug before the first import,
because the slug is also the public URL.

Worth noting the orphan was the more dangerous of the two: it was the version of
the page whose content had been rejected, and it stayed reachable.

### Why the first drop script did not work

Three faults, all mine.

1. **Drafts.** Every Sanity document can have a `drafts.<id>` twin holding its
   own copy of every reference. Unlinking the published issue leaves the draft
   issue still pointing at the article, which still blocks the delete. Both have
   to be unlinked, and the draft article deleted before the published one.
2. **Order.** Unlink and delete ran as separate requests. A Studio session
   saving the issue in between restores the reference. They are now one
   transaction, which Sanity applies all or nothing.
3. **Silence.** It caught the Sanity error and printed its own summary. The API
   response names the document still holding the reference, which is the only
   part worth reading. It is now printed verbatim.

`npm run drop` also takes several slugs at once.

## The Missionary Story, again

The Livingstone piece is a word-for-word transcription of the entry by
**R. Grundemann in the New Schaff-Herzog Encyclopedia of Religious Knowledge,
1910** ([ccel.org](https://www.ccel.org/s/schaff/encyc/encyc07/htm/ii.i.iv.htm)),
down to the encyclopedia's own run-in headings, its opening headword line, and
its scan artefacts. The tilde in "Kolobeñ" is an OCR error in the CCEL text and
it is reproduced in the magazine, which settles the direction of copying beyond
argument.

The 1910 text is public domain, so nothing was stolen in the property sense. It
still ran under Elizabeth Clayton's byline as our own work. That is the fourth
Missionary Story in this archive found to be somebody else's text.

**Rewritten**, per `MISSIONARY-STORIES.md`, from Livingstone Online's edition of
his manuscripts, the Birthplace Museum, the One More Voice project, Blaikie
(1880), Parsons (1998), Helly (1987), Lewis (2018) and the Royal Geographical
Society. **2,036 words against the column's 1,600 ceiling.** That is well over and I have
left it over rather than pretend otherwise. Two passes of tightening took out
only 36 words, which is the signal that the length is the material and not the
prose: the childhood, Sechele, the Zambezi, Nyangwe, the thousand-mile march,
the Bombay and Nasik connection and the colonial criticism are each load-bearing,
and the only real cut available is the honest-criticism section, which is the
one part that must not go. **Premasis's call whether to run it long or drop a
section.**

### What the research changed

Four things I had assumed turned out to be wrong, and the corrections all made
the piece better:

- The oft-repeated **piggery** at Shuttle Row is unsourceable anywhere. Dropped.
- **Jacob Wainwright was not excluded from the pallbearers.** He was one of them,
  reported at the time by the *Penny Illustrated Paper*. The real indignity is
  elsewhere: he was called a boy and made to change out of his green jacket.
- Livingstone did not **excommunicate** Sechele. He wrote "cut him off for a
  season", and explicitly judged that he had not apostatised completely.
- **Susi carved the tree**, not Wainwright.

### The find that makes this an Indian story

**Chuma was schooled in Bombay in 1864-65. Jacob Wainwright was schooled by the
Church Missionary Society at Nasik.** The Royal Geographical Society calls this
cohort the Bombay Africans: people enslaved in Africa, freed by the Royal Navy,
landed in western India, educated there, and recruited back into African
expeditions. At least thirty are identifiable.

The men who carried Livingstone's body a thousand miles to the coast over nine
months were educated in Maharashtra. Their instruction before sailing was
explicit: if he were dead, find his grave and bring back his bones. The
Westminster Abbey gravestone reads BROUGHT BY FAITHFUL HANDS OVER LAND AND SEA
and names none of them.

That is the strongest reason for this magazine to run a Livingstone piece at
all, and the 1910 encyclopedia entry does not mention Chuma or Susi by name.

### The Zanzibar claim, corrected

The standard telling is that Livingstone's reporting closed the Zanzibar slave
market five weeks after his death. The piece declines that. The British envoy
had already been refused in January 1873, months before Livingstone died, and
the signature was obtained in June under an explicit threat of naval blockade.
Dorothy Helly's formulation is the accurate one: his account fed into a campaign
already under way. And the market closed while slavery itself continued in
Zanzibar for another quarter century.

### Why he is worth an Indian magazine's Heritage page

Because of what he actually built here, which the plagiarised entry mentioned in

authority for the Indian Civil Service Hindi examination. **Tej Bhatia's standard
history of the field names a forty-four-year period after him: the Kellogg Era,

Rev. J. J. Caleb of the Mission Press, thanked for the printing. No Indian member

enormously over-populated, wrote that before British rule incessant war had
usefully kept the numbers down, and concluded that British administration was
probably more practically Christian than any other in the world.

### What now stops it happening again: `npm run check`

`seed/check-articles.mjs`, run over every article file. The check that matters is
TRUNCATION, and the tell was sitting in the data all along: **the last paragraph
did not end in a full stop.**

It flags a paragraph anywhere in a body that does not end in terminal
punctuation, a body that ends on a heading with nothing beneath it, and a final
paragraph trailing off on a conjunction or preposition. It also catches unknown
block types, bad note placements, pull-quote highlights that are not inside their
quote, missing rights clearance on archive pieces, and authors declared nowhere.

Three legitimate patterns are deliberately allowed, because a check that cries
wolf gets turned off: a paragraph ending in a colon that introduces a verse
block, a scripture reference printed after the closing quotation mark, and an
attribution dash before a name.

Run across all 56 articles in 18 files: **no faults.** Fourteen import-order
dependencies are reported as information rather than errors, since shared
contributors are deliberately declared once and referenced from the other issues
they wrote for. Those do not fail the run.

Run it before every import.

## The Gandhi article: two separate problems

**First, it is copied.** The text is a near-verbatim reprint of **Dibin Samuel,
"Mahatma Gandhi and Christianity", *Christian Today India*, 14 August 2008**
([christiantoday.co.in](https://www.christiantoday.co.in/article/mahatma.gandhi.and.christianity/2837.htm)),
running under Joseph Dias's byline. Every quotation appears there, in the same
order, with the same wording and the same ellipses. The same text was recycled
elsewhere, including a reprint credited to *The Messenger*, the Archdiocese of
Bombay's weekly.

**Joseph Dias is a real, identifiable living person** — General Secretary of the
Catholic Secular Forum in Mumbai — which makes this worse rather than better.
Publishing another writer's article under his name exposes him as well as the
magazine.

**Second, and independent of the copying, one of the anecdotes is false.**

The article says Gandhi was turned away from a South African church, that an
Englishman asked "Where do you think you're going, kaffir?", that an elder said
"There's no room for kaffirs in this church", and that this "forced Gandhi to
never again consider being a Christian".

Gandhi's own *Autobiography* says the opposite. He attended a Wesleyan church in
Pretoria regularly and was admitted; his objection was that the congregation
struck him as "worldly-minded people going to church for recreation and in
conformity to custom". His reasons for not converting are set out at length and
are theological: he could not accept Jesus as the only incarnation, or the
doctrine of the atonement. He went on with Christian enquiry afterwards, with
Baker and Coates. The word "kaffir" was a slur applied to Black Africans, not to
Indians, which makes the dialogue internally implausible as well.

**And the famous quotation is apocryphal.** "I don't reject Christ. I love
Christ. It's just that so many of you Christians are so unlike Christ" has no
authoritative source. E. Stanley Jones published *Mahatma Gandhi: An
Interpretation* in 1948 including his correspondence with Gandhi, and this
conversation is not in it. Snopes finds no authoritative link; the earliest trace
is a 1927 *Harvard Crimson* report where a similar line is a third party's
summary of Gandhi's view, not his words.

The bitter irony of the page is that its **fabricated** dialogue is printed inside
quotation marks with full confidence, while the material Jones actually did
record, Gandhi's four-point answer on naturalising Christianity in India, is the
part that has been loosely paraphrased.

Held, twice over.
